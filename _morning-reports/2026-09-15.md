## Top 3 Things Worth Paying Attention To

### 1. OpenAI cracked a $1M Millennium Prize Problem with AI agents — and the data privacy controversy around it is the real story

**What it is:** OpenAI used an unreleased model to resolve the Navier–Stokes existence and smoothness problem — one of seven Millennium Prize Problems carrying a $1M prize. The agents ran for 88 hours, sent 2.7 million messages, and used ~130 billion output tokens. But here's the twist: an Anthropic employee (mathematician Levent Alpöge) and NYU professor Tristan Buckmaster had spent nearly a year working on the same problem using OpenAI's own Codex tools — storing all their drafts in Codex sessions. OpenAI says it learned from "rumors" and can't rule out that de-identified training data from those sessions influenced the model that solved it.

**Why it matters:** This is the clearest real-world demonstration yet of what AI data training privacy actually means in practice. Simon Willison's framing cuts to it: "If I use ChatGPT to help me partially solve a Millennium Prize problem, what are the chances that my work will influence training such that a later model helps someone else solve it first?" That question now has a plausible answer, not just a hypothetical one. For anyone doing serious creative or research work in AI tools, this changes the calculus on what to put in a prompt.

### 2. Shopify dropped React Native after 6 years because AI agents changed the math

**What it is:** Shopify is moving back to separate Swift and Kotlin native codebases from React Native. They adopted React Native in 2020 specifically to stop building features twice and let developers work across the stack. Their explanation: "Agents can now do enough of the implementation, translation, testing, and review work that it's no longer the deciding factor it was in 2020."

**Why it matters:** This is one of the most concrete, publicly documented examples of AI changing an actual engineering decision — not a startup pivoting or a vague claim, but a major company reversing a six-year architectural choice because the underlying cost assumption changed. The assumption was: maintaining two native codebases is expensive enough to justify the React Native tradeoffs. AI just eliminated that assumption. This same logic will hit other "write once, run anywhere" or "consolidate tooling" decisions across engineering.

**Reel angle:** "Shopify just reversed a 6-year technical decision because of AI — and the reason shows exactly what agents are changing about software engineering"

### 3. Harness engineering is now its own discipline — and it's where the real reliability gains are

**What it is:** Multiple high-signal sources in this week's AI practitioner discourse converged on the same point: production AI reliability comes from the harness (permissions, tool routing, kill switches, memory management, retries, verifiers) not from which model you pick. Specific data point: LangChain noted a file-reading format change reduced `edit_file` errors by 15% and total input tokens by 10%. A practical guide from Omar Shorbagy laid out the principle: separate inference, tools, and loop; keep prompts minimal; log aggressively; layer in memory, skills, and subagents only after the core loop works. The AI Engineer World's Fair Harness Engineering track made the same case from the production angle: when agents fail, it's usually not "the model" — it's tool routing, permissions, memory, and kill switches.

**Why it matters:** Most of Mansi's audience is still optimizing at the "which model is best" layer. The people actually shipping AI systems have moved on. This is the single most repeatable unlock pattern for going from "I use ChatGPT" to "I have a workflow that reliably does X." It's also the answer to why well-designed smaller setups sometimes outperform throwing GPT-6 Astra at a task with no structure.

**Format:** Explainer

**Reel angle:** "Everyone's obsessing over which model to use — the people shipping real AI systems figured out it's never the model"
