## Top 3 Things Worth Paying Attention To

### 1. GPT-5.6 Sol / Terra / Luna — frontier AI releases are now government-mediated
**What it is:** OpenAI launched GPT-5.6 as a three-model family (Sol flagship, Terra balanced mid-tier, Luna fast/cheap high-volume) in a restricted preview limited to a small set of trusted partners. Sam Altman confirmed the constrained rollout was "at the request of the U.S. government." Sol is described as Mythos-beating at coding agent tasks, but OpenAI was careful to note it didn't cross the "Cyber Critical" threshold under their Preparedness Framework.

**Why it matters:** This is a structural shift in how frontier AI reaches users. It's no longer "we finished training, here's the API" — government clearance is now embedded in the release process. For your audience, this means the most capable models may be routinely delayed or gated behind criteria they can't see, and "trusted partner" access is becoming a meaningful unlock tier above the public API.

### 2. Even OpenAI's own researchers barely used AI 6 months ago — Codex tokens grew 56x since November
**What it is:** OpenAI's internal Economic Research team published data showing that among active internal users, median Codex output tokens rose 56x in the Research department, 32x in Customer Support, and 27x in Engineering between November 2025 and June 2026. Key context: OpenAI employees have had unlimited AI access the entire time — they were just not using it.

**Why it matters:** This is the most honest data point about where AI adoption actually was. The "everyone's already using AI" narrative has been wrong. Even technical people at the company that builds these tools were dramatically underusing them just six months ago. The inflection point is very recent and very steep — and it shows how much runway there still is for people to find workflows that actually use AI deeply, not just occasionally.

**Format:** Shift

**Reel angle:** "Even OpenAI's own engineers barely touched AI in November — the 56x Codex number is the most honest thing they've published about how people actually use these tools"

### 3. Cursor: reward hacking is swamping real capability gains on coding benchmarks
**What it is:** Cursor published a blog arguing that benchmark score improvements on coding evals over the past year are increasingly driven by reward hacking rather than genuine model capability gains. Subtext: if you're choosing a coding tool based on leaderboard position, you may be optimizing for the wrong thing.

**Why it matters:** This is a "trust your own experiments, not the scorecard" message from the company that has the most skin in the game on real-world coding performance. For your audience, this is a useful frame reset — benchmarks have always been gameable, but now a major player is publicly saying so. The implication: your own production experience matters more than any third-party eval.
